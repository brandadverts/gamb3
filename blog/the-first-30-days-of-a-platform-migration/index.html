The first 30 days of a platform migration

Most migrations slip for reasons that are not technical — the order of the work is wrong.

The Gamb Gaming teamNovember 16, 20263 min read

Moving a live operation onto new infrastructure is harder than building from scratch. Building from scratch, you only look forward. In a migration you also have to look behind you — at the balances, the open bet slips and the half-finished withdrawal requests you are carrying across.

The thirty days below are not a delivery promise. They are a frame for how the work is ordered. The real duration depends on the number of integrations, the volume of data and what the licence requires.

Week 1 — Inventory

No code is written in the first week. What gets written is the list of what moves.

  • How many player records are there, how many are fully verified, how many are restricted?
  • Open bet slips, pending withdrawals, bonuses mid-wagering: what happens to each at cut-over?
  • Which provider contracts exist, and which of them continue on the new platform?
  • Which reports go to whom? Most of them are read by nobody. A migration is a good moment to

retire them.

The output is a single table: moving, rebuilding, leaving behind.

Week 2 — Data rehearsal

Data is rehearsed at least twice before go-live. A rehearsal is not "we copied the data"; it is comparing the copy against the old system record by record.

Three things to check:

  1. Balance totals. Does the total in the old system match the new one to the cent?
  2. Record counts. Do player, transaction and audit record counts line up?
  3. Edge cases. Negative balances, closed accounts, duplicates, incomplete verification —

what happens to each when it moves?

Finding a gap in rehearsal is good news. The bad news is finding it in production.

Week 3 — Parallel run

The new platform comes up while the old one keeps serving. The operations team rehearses real scenarios on the new screens: approve a withdrawal, set up a bonus, close a case. This is not a training week; training is a by-product of the rehearsal.

The same week, the rollback steps get written. "We'll roll back if something goes wrong" is not a plan. The plan is: at which threshold, who decides, what is switched off in what order, and where the data comes back from. A rollback plan that is not written down does not exist.

Week 4 — Opening the traffic

Traffic is never opened all at once. Internal team first, then a small cohort of players, then one market, then the rest. At every step you watch the same four numbers: balance variance, error rate, support ticket volume, payment approval rate.

The most common mistake on launch day is switching platform and bringing a new provider live at the same time. When something breaks you cannot tell which of the two caused it. Provider changes wait at least two weeks after the migration.

Day thirty

What you should hold at the end of day thirty is more than a live platform: a closed reconciliation, a trained team, a written rollback plan and a list of what is still in the old system. The old system is not switched off immediately — audit records and retention rules mean it stays readable for a while.

The most useful question while planning a migration is this: if this step goes wrong, how many minutes until we notice? Any step without an answer goes back into the plan.
